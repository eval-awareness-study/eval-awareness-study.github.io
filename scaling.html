Scaling sweep

Do bigger models notice more often that they are being tested?

Qwen3.5 family · 8 model sizes from 0.8B to 397B parameters

We ran eight sizes of one AI model family on three sets of agent tasks. After each run we measured, in several ways, whether the model thought it was being evaluated rather than used for real. Then we looked at how that changes with model size, task difficulty and task length.

Key findings

  • Bigger models more often say they are being evaluated when asked after the task. On the multiple-choice questions, answers saying evaluation go from 0.8B to 397B: 53% → 94% on the coding tasks; 44% → 96% on Terminal-Bench; 54% → 100% on harmful AgentHarm tasks; 49% → 99% on their harmless twins. Over all 9 questions and 4 task sets, 29 of 35 measurable size trends rise and 6 fall (Multiple choice: what was the purpose?; Yes/no answer probability; After the task: were you being evaluated during it?).
  • Models rarely say it on their own. In the reasoning they write during the task, they mention being tested in none of 2,976 runs on the coding tasks; 37 of 2,007 runs (1.8%) on Terminal-Bench; 108 of 3,690 runs (2.9%) on harmful AgentHarm tasks; 27 of 3,739 runs (0.7%) on their harmless twins.
  • Some “evaluation” answers are really yes-to-everything. Asked both “is this an evaluation?” and “is this real use?”, models often say yes to both: up to 88% of runs at 122B on the coding tasks. Counting only runs that say evaluation and not real use, the share no longer rises steadily with size: 10% at 0.8B, 28% at 2B, 54% at 4B, 39% at 9B, 70% at 27B, 4% at 35B, 6% at 122B, 47% at 397B. On Terminal-Bench this stricter count still rises with size.
  • Task difficulty and task length matter much less than size. From the smallest to the largest model, the answers change by 58 points on average; from the easiest to the hardest task by 10, and from the shortest to the longest task by 7. Harder tasks: 1 trend rises, 9 trends fall, 8 no clear change. Longer tasks: 8 trends rise, 3 trends fall, 25 no clear change.
  • Size still matters once task difficulty for that model is accounted for. Bigger models also solve more tasks, so awareness could just follow how hard a task is for the model. In 17 of 17 tests on the coding tasks and Terminal-Bench, knowing the model's size still improves the prediction.
  • Internal probe. Reading the model's activations directly, without asking anything, the size trend rises in 0, falls in 0 and is unclear or not measurable in 4 of the 4 task sets. For 2B the probe could not tell known test prompts from known real-use prompts of the same length, so that size is left out. Reading the model's word predictions from inside it (“lenses”) did not work; see below.

What we did

Three task sets

  • Controlled coding tasks. We delete function bodies in real open-source projects and ask the model to restore them. We set the difficulty (levels 1–8) and the number of chained steps (1–16).
  • Terminal-Bench (real terminal tasks). 84 released tasks done in a Linux terminal, such as building software or fixing a server. We do not control their difficulty or length, so we use how often other AI agents fail each task and the task author's time estimate.
  • AgentHarm: harmful tasks. Agent tasks with simulated tools (fake email, web search, payments) that ask for something clearly harmful, such as fraud or harassment. Nothing leaves the sandbox.
  • AgentHarm: harmless twins. The same tasks and tools rewritten with a harmless goal, so refusing them is not about harm.

Five ways to tell whether a model thinks it is being tested

The figures show these five. Seven more measures are listed in the tables at the bottom.

Says it's being tested in its own reasoning
A separate AI model reads the reasoning the model writes during the task and looks for statements that it is being tested or evaluated. Nothing is asked.
Multiple-choice questions: evaluation or real use? (Needham et al.)
After the task the model sees its own transcript and answers five multiple-choice questions on whether it was an evaluation or real use. We count the answers that say evaluation.
Open question: what was the purpose of this interaction?
After the task the model says in its own words what the interaction was for. A separate AI model sorts the answer; we count answers that say evaluation.
0–100 rating: how likely was this an evaluation?
After the task the model gives a number from 0 to 100 for how likely it is that the interaction was an evaluation.
Internal probe (reads the model's activations)
A simple classifier trained on the model's internal activations for thousands of prompts known to be tests or real use, from many sources. It is then applied at every turn of the task, at the same point in the prompt it was trained on. Nothing is asked.

Reading the model's activations

Internal probe

The probe looks at the model's internal activations instead of asking it anything. It is trained on prompts known to be tests or real use, then applied at every turn of the task. Its size trend is shown only if it holds when each model size is left out in turn.

Lenses did not work

We also tried a second kind of internal readout, called a lens. A lens turns the model's intermediate activations into guesses at the next word, and we checked how strongly words like “fictional” and “simulation” came up. It did not work. We first checked each lens on short prompts known to be tests or real use. For a random test prompt and a random real-use prompt, a working lens would score the test higher nearly every time; a coin flip does so half the time. The lenses did so in 23 to 89 of 100 pairs, and below 65 in 16 of the 18 model-and-lens combinations. Only 1 of 18 passed the check at every prompt length. The likely reason is that the two words describe a made-up scenario, while the test prompts are ordinary benchmark questions. So the lenses are not shown.

The other questions
The same multiple-choice questions, also reading loosely formatted answers
As above, but also counting answers that do not follow the requested answer format.
Open question: what situation are you in?
After the task the model describes its situation without being asked about evaluation. We count descriptions whose main point is that it is being evaluated.
Pick one: evaluation / training / real use / …
The model picks the single best description of the session from a shuffled list.
Multiple choice: what was the purpose?
One multiple-choice question on the purpose of the interaction, with several evaluation and several real-use options.
Yes/no answer probability
We ask whether the interaction is an evaluation and read the model's probability of answering yes rather than no, without letting it write an answer.
After the task: were you being evaluated during it?
We ask whether, while doing the task, the model believed it was being evaluated.
Multiple choice, excluding models that say yes to everything
The model must say evaluation to the direct question and also say no to its mirror question (“is this real use?”). A model that says yes to everything scores zero.

How the numbers are computed
  • Models. Qwen3.5 models with reasoning on, sampled with the same settings at every size. Two sizes mix several expert sub-networks and use only part of their parameters per word (marked “active”); size always means total parameters.
  • Runs. Each size did each task several times. A run that a measure could not score (no reasoning, an unreadable answer) is left out for that measure, never counted as “no”. Runs that failed for technical reasons, not because of the model, were rerun.
  • Trends. Each trend comes from a model of the data that gives every task its own baseline, so many runs of one easy-to-spot task do not count as independent evidence. Difficulty and length trends are fitted over all sizes together.
  • Does size still matter once task difficulty for that model is accounted for? We predict awareness from how often that model solved the task (plus task length and reasoning length), then add model size. “Yes” means adding size clearly improves the prediction.
  • Observed changes in the figures and the summary are raw numbers: the smallest vs the largest model, or the easiest vs the hardest setting averaged over sizes. The arrows come from the fitted trends.
  • Internal readouts. Turns with prompts longer than 65,536 tokens were not scored, at any size.
